Bosons, Fermions, and the Many-Body Hilbert Space#

Hilbert Space of Particles#

Consider a single particle with Hilbert space spanned by position kets \(\lvert x\rangle\).

Usually one takes \(x \in \mathbb{R}\), but in AMO or condensed-matter settings one may also consider \(x \in \mathbb{Z}\), for example for atoms in an optical lattice.

This distinction will not matter much in what follows, except that one writes either integrals or sums:

\[ \mathbb{I} = \int dx\, \lvert x\rangle \langle x\rvert \qquad \text{vs.} \qquad \mathbb{I} = \sum_x \lvert x\rangle \langle x\rvert \]

and likewise

\[ \langle x \mid y \rangle = \delta(x-y) \qquad \text{vs.} \qquad \langle x \mid y \rangle = \delta_{x,y}. \]

In these notes we use the discrete notation.

For a state \(\lvert \psi \rangle\), the wavefunction in this basis is

\[ \psi(x) = \langle x \mid \psi \rangle. \]

For two distinguishable particles, the Hilbert space is spanned by

\[ \lvert x_1, x_2\rangle = \lvert x_1\rangle \otimes \lvert x_2\rangle, \]

with wavefunction

\[ \psi(x_1,x_2)=\langle x_1,x_2 \mid \psi\rangle. \]

Similarly, for \(N\) distinguishable particles one has the wavefunction

\[ \psi(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_N)=\langle x_1,x_2,\dots,x_N \mid \psi\rangle. \]

For distinguishable particles this wavefunction lives in the full tensor-product Hilbert space. For identical particles, however, the physical Hilbert space is the symmetric subspace (bosons) or antisymmetric subspace (fermions) of that tensor-product space.

The key physical point is that observables depend on probabilities and expectation values, such as \(|\psi(x_1,x_2,\dots)|^2\), not directly on the complex amplitude itself. Exchanging two identical particles may therefore change the wavefunction by a phase without changing any observable. For ordinary particles, doing the same exchange twice must return the original state, so that phase can only be \(+1\) or \(-1\). This gives the two possibilities: bosons with symmetric wavefunctions and fermions with antisymmetric wavefunctions.

Exchange Symmetry for Identical Bosons#

A basic physical fact is that if the particles belong to the same species, then exchanging their labels does not produce a new physical state. In first quantization, this means that physical states must lie in the symmetric subspace.

Equivalently,

\[ \hat{P}_{ij}\lvert \psi \rangle = \lvert \psi \rangle \]

for every exchange operator \(\hat{P}_{ij}\). Equivalently, in the position representation the bosonic wavefunction satisfies

\[ \psi(\dots,x_i,\dots,x_j,\dots)=\psi(\dots,x_j,\dots,x_i,\dots). \]

This is exchange symmetry, and it has several important consequences.

Consequences for observables#

Any physical observable should be represented by an operator \(\hat{O}\) that does not distinguish particle labels. Thus, for example,

\[ \langle x_1,x_2 \mid \hat{O} \mid x_1,x_2\rangle = \langle x_2,x_1 \mid \hat{O} \mid x_2,x_1\rangle. \]

There is no way to measure which boson is at \(x\); only that a boson is at \(x\).

Put differently, the measurable quantity is the probability density \(|\psi|^2\), not the amplitude \(\psi\) itself. A minus sign in the wavefunction under exchange therefore does not by itself change the probability density, which is why antisymmetric fermionic states are physically allowed alongside symmetric bosonic ones.

Equivalently, physical observables must respect exchange symmetry, i.e. they must commute with all exchange operators \(\hat{P}_{ij}\).

For example, the local number operator \(\hat{n}(x)\) counts how many particles occupy the single-particle state \(x\). In a two-particle state its expectation value is schematically

\[ \langle \hat{n}(x) \rangle = \sum_{x_2}\psi^*(x,x_2)\psi(x,x_2) + \sum_{x_1}\psi^*(x_1,x)\psi(x_1,x). \]

Exchange and Interference#

Exchange symmetry allows constructive interference.

In quantum mechanics, the amplitude to evolve from an initial configuration \(1,2\) to a final configuration \(1',2'\) is a sum over all paths connecting the initial and final states.

For bosons, amplitudes associated with exchanged particle labels contribute to the same physical final state, and therefore add with the same sign.

This has measurable consequences, for example in two-particle interference effects such as the Hanbury-Brown–Twiss effect.

Bosons in First-Quantized Language#

Wavefunctions and Exchange Symmetry#

In first quantization we consider wavefunctions

\[ \psi(x_1,x_2,\dots) \]

and impose bosonic symmetry:

\[ \psi(\dots,x_i,\dots,x_j,\dots) = \psi(\dots,x_j,\dots,x_i,\dots). \]

Equivalently, if \(\hat{P}_{ij}\) denotes the operator that swaps particle labels \(i \leftrightarrow j\), then

\[ \hat{P}_{ij}\psi = \psi. \]

Is this consistent with time evolution? Yes, because any physically admissible Hamiltonian must preserve exchange symmetry:

\[ \hat{H} = \sum_i \frac{\hat{p}_i^2}{2m} + \sum_i V(\hat{x}_i) + \frac12 \sum_{i\neq j} U(\hat{x}_i-\hat{x}_j) + \cdots \]

and

\[ \hat{P}_{ij} \hat{H} \hat{P}_{ij} = \hat{H}. \]

Therefore, if

\[ i\partial_t \psi = \hat{H}\psi \]

and \(\hat{P}_{ij}\psi(0)=\psi(0)\), then \(\hat{P}_{ij}\psi(t)=\psi(t)\) for all \(t\).

For example, one must forbid terms like

\[ V_1(x_1)+V_2(x_2) \qquad \text{with } V_1\neq V_2, \]

because those distinguish particle 1 from particle 2.

Bosonic Occupation-Number Basis#

Because of exchange symmetry, it is natural to expand states using symmetrized basis states.

For two particles,

\[\begin{split} \lvert x_1,x_2\rangle_+ = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left(\lvert x_1,x_2\rangle + \lvert x_2,x_1\rangle\right), & x_1\neq x_2,\\[1em] \lvert x_1,x_1\rangle, & x_1=x_2. \end{cases} \end{split}\]

More generally,

\[ \lvert \{x_i\}\rangle_+ = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal N}} \sum_{\text{perm}} \lvert \text{Perm}\{x_i\}\rangle, \]

where the sum runs over all permutations of the list \(\{x_i\}\) and \(\mathcal N\) is the appropriate normalization factor.

Since the ordering no longer matters, we instead label states by the occupation numbers \(n_x\), where \(n_x\) is the number of particles occupying the single-particle state \(x\).

For example, for \(N=2\) particles and orbitals \(x=1,2,3\):

\[ \lvert 1,3\rangle_+ \;\leftrightarrow\; \lvert n_1=1,n_2=0,n_3=1\rangle = \lvert 101\rangle, \]
\[ \lvert 1,2\rangle_+ \;\leftrightarrow\; \lvert n_1=1,n_2=1,n_3=0\rangle = \lvert 110\rangle, \]
\[ \lvert 1,1\rangle_+ \;\leftrightarrow\; \lvert n_1=2,n_2=0,n_3=0\rangle = \lvert 200\rangle. \]

The state \(\lvert \{n_x\}\rangle\) is called an occupation-number basis state.

The total particle number operator is

\[ \hat{N} = \sum_x \hat{n}_x. \]

Dependence on the Single-Particle Basis#

The occupation basis depends on the choice of single-particle basis.

Instead of the basis \(\lvert x\rangle\), we could use another basis \(\lvert k\rangle\):

\[ \lvert x\rangle \to \lvert k\rangle, \qquad \lvert x_1,x_2,\dots\rangle \to \lvert k_1,k_2,\dots\rangle, \qquad \lvert \{n_x\}\rangle \to \lvert \{n_k\}\rangle. \]

Using, for example,

\[ \langle x \mid k\rangle = \frac{e^{ikx}}{\sqrt{L}}, \]

one can work out overlaps such as

\[ \langle x_1,x_2,\dots \mid k_1,k_2,\dots\rangle. \]

Computing overlaps between bosonic occupation states in different bases can become nontrivial; mathematically this is related to matrix permanents and, in complexity-theory language, to the boson sampling problem.

Noninteracting Bosons#

Suppose the Hamiltonian is noninteracting:

\[ \hat{H} = \sum_{i=1}^N \left(\frac{\hat{p}_i^2}{2m}+V(\hat{x}_i)\right). \]

Let the single-particle eigenstates satisfy

\[ \left(\frac{\hat{p}^2}{2m}+V(\hat{x})\right)\lvert \alpha\rangle = E_\alpha \lvert \alpha\rangle. \]

Then the many-body Hilbert space may be described either in first-quantized form, \(\lvert \alpha_1,\alpha_2,\dots,\alpha_N\rangle\) or in occupation-number form, \(\lvert \{n_\alpha\}\rangle\).

In the occupation basis,

\[ \hat{H} = \sum_\alpha E_\alpha \hat{n}_\alpha, \]

where

\[ \hat{n}_\alpha \lvert n_0,n_1,n_2,\dots\rangle = n_\alpha \lvert n_0,n_1,n_2,\dots\rangle. \]

We can equivalently write the Hamiltonian in terms of ladder operators,

\[ \hat{H} = \sum_\alpha E_\alpha \hat{a}_\alpha^\dagger \hat{a}_\alpha. \]

Thus the noninteracting bosonic problem is formally equivalent to a collection of decoupled harmonic oscillators, with oscillator energy \(E_\alpha\) and occupation number operator \(\hat{n}_\alpha=\hat{a}_\alpha^\dagger \hat{a}_\alpha\).

Ladder operators should be familiar to you, but I describe their essence in the following note.

Note

Bosonic creation and annihilation operators

Define bosonic ladder operators \(\hat{a}_\alpha^\dagger\) and \(\hat{a}_\alpha\) by

\[ \hat{n}_\alpha = \hat{a}_\alpha^\dagger \hat{a}_\alpha, \]

with commutation relations

\[ [\hat{a}_\alpha,\hat{a}_\beta^\dagger]=\delta_{\alpha\beta}, \]

and all other commutators vanishing. Their action on occupation states is

\[ \hat{a}_\alpha^\dagger \lvert \dots,n_\alpha,\dots\rangle = \sqrt{n_\alpha+1}\, \lvert \dots,n_\alpha+1,\dots\rangle, \]
\[ \hat{a}_\alpha \lvert \dots,n_\alpha,\dots\rangle = \sqrt{n_\alpha}\, \lvert \dots,n_\alpha-1,\dots\rangle. \]

Thus, \(\hat{a}_\alpha^\dagger\) creates a boson in state \(\alpha\), while \(\hat{a}_\alpha\) annihilates a boson in state \(\alpha\).

A quick derivation of the standard relations is as follows. If we define the action of \(\hat{a}_\alpha^\dagger\) and \(\hat{a}_\alpha\) on occupation states by the formulas above, then

\[ \hat{a}_\alpha^\dagger \hat{a}_\alpha \lvert \dots,n_\alpha,\dots\rangle = n_\alpha \lvert \dots,n_\alpha,\dots\rangle, \]

so indeed

\[ \hat{n}_\alpha = \hat{a}_\alpha^\dagger \hat{a}_\alpha. \]

Likewise,

\[ \hat{a}_\alpha \hat{a}_\alpha^\dagger \lvert \dots,n_\alpha,\dots\rangle = (n_\alpha+1) \lvert \dots,n_\alpha,\dots\rangle, \]

and therefore

\[ [\hat{a}_\alpha,\hat{a}_\alpha^\dagger]\lvert \dots,n_\alpha,\dots\rangle = \lvert \dots,n_\alpha,\dots\rangle. \]

Since this holds on every occupation-number basis state, we get

\[ [\hat{a}_\alpha,\hat{a}_\beta^\dagger] = \delta_{\alpha\beta}. \]

Under a single-particle unitary change of basis,

\[ \lvert \alpha\rangle = \sum_x U_{\alpha x}\lvert x\rangle, \]

the creation operators transform as

\[ \hat{a}_\alpha^\dagger = \sum_x U_{\alpha x}\, \hat{a}_x^\dagger. \]

A noninteracting Hamiltonian can be written as

\[ \hat{H} = \sum_{x,y} \hat{a}_x^\dagger H_{xy} \hat{a}_y = \sum_\alpha E_\alpha \hat{a}_\alpha^\dagger \hat{a}_\alpha. \]

Here \(H_{xy} = \langle x \mid \hat{H}_{\mathrm{1p}} \mid y \rangle\) are the matrix elements of the single-particle Hamiltonian.

Grand-Canonical Statistical Mechanics of Noninteracting Bosons#

For

\[ \hat{H} = \sum_\alpha E_\alpha \hat{n}_\alpha, \]

the grand-canonical partition function is simple because all number operators commute:

\[ [\hat{n}_\alpha,\hat{n}_\beta]=0. \]

Then

\[ e^{-\beta(\hat{H}-\mu \hat{N})} = e^{-\beta\sum_\alpha (E_\alpha-\mu)\hat{n}_\alpha}, \]

and

\[ Z = \sum_{\{n_\alpha\}} e^{-\beta\sum_\alpha (E_\alpha-\mu)n_\alpha} = \prod_\alpha \left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} e^{-\beta n(E_\alpha-\mu)}\right) = \prod_\alpha \frac{1}{1-e^{-\beta(E_\alpha-\mu)}}. \]

This expression converges only if the chemical potential lies below the lowest single-particle energy, i.e. \(\mu < \min_\alpha E_\alpha\).

Therefore,

\[ \langle n_\alpha\rangle = \frac{1}{e^{\beta(E_\alpha-\mu)}-1}, \]

which is the Bose-Einstein statistics, which we’ve already encountered in our discussion of phonons.

Note

Phonons also obey Bose-Einstein statistics, but with an important simplification: their chemical potential is effectively \(\mu=0\). The reason is that phonon number is not conserved; the crystal can create or absorb phonons as it exchanges energy internally. So for a phonon mode of frequency \(\omega_k\),

\[ \langle n_k\rangle = \frac{1}{e^{\beta \hbar \omega_k}-1}, \]

which is just the Bose-Einstein distribution evaluated at zero chemical potential.

Fermions: Antisymmetry and Pauli Exclusion#

For fermions, physical states lie in the antisymmetric subspace, so

\[ \hat{P}_{ij}\psi = -\psi, \]

so for two particles

\[ \psi(x_1,x_2) = -\psi(x_2,x_1). \]

The properly antisymmetrized state is

\[ \lvert x_1,x_2,\dots\rangle_- = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal N}} \sum_{\text{perm}} (-1)^p \lvert \text{Perm}\{x_i\}\rangle, \]

where \(p\) is the parity of the permutation.

Example:

\[ \lvert 1,2\rangle_- = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left(\lvert 1,2\rangle - \lvert 2,1\rangle\right). \]

If any two coordinates are equal, the antisymmetrized state vanishes:

\[ \lvert \{x_i\}\rangle_- = 0 \qquad \text{if any } x_i=x_j. \]

This is the Pauli exclusion principle.

Hence one may restrict to ordered representatives such as

\[ x_1 < x_2 < x_3 < \cdots \]

and use an occupation basis again, but now

\[ n_x \in \{0,1\}. \]

Note

Exclusion as destructive interference

Pauli exclusion can be understood heuristically as a destructive interference effect: in a path-integral picture, contributions related by exchanging two identical fermions come with opposite signs, so when the two particles are forced into the same one-particle state those amplitudes cancel.

More fundamentally, however, exclusion follows directly from antisymmetry of the fermionic wavefunction,

\[ \psi(x_1,x_2) = -\psi(x_2,x_1). \]

For spinless fermions this implies

\[ \psi(x,x)=0, \]

so the amplitude vanishes when the two particles are brought to the same position. More generally, the Pauli principle says that two identical fermions cannot occupy the same one-particle quantum state.

Grand-Canonical Statistical Mechanics of Noninteracting Fermions#

For noninteracting fermions,

\[ \hat{H}=\sum_\alpha E_\alpha \hat{n}_\alpha, \qquad n_\alpha=0,1. \]

Thus

\[ Z = \sum_{\{n_\alpha\}} e^{-\beta\sum_\alpha (E_\alpha-\mu)n_\alpha} = \prod_\alpha \left(\sum_{n=0}^1 e^{-\beta(E_\alpha-\mu)n}\right) = \prod_\alpha \left(1+e^{-\beta(E_\alpha-\mu)}\right). \]

Therefore,

\[ \langle n_\alpha\rangle = \frac{e^{-\beta(E_\alpha-\mu)}}{1+e^{-\beta(E_\alpha-\mu)}} = \frac{1}{e^{\beta(E_\alpha-\mu)}+1}, \]

which is the Fermi–Dirac statistics.

Fermionic Creation and Annihilation Operators#

Define fermionic ladder operators \(\hat{c}_\alpha^\dagger\), \(\hat{c}_\alpha\) and number operator

\[ \hat{n}_\alpha = \hat{c}_\alpha^\dagger \hat{c}_\alpha. \]

For a single orbital, with basis states \(\lvert 0\rangle\) and \(\lvert 1\rangle\),

\[ \hat{c}^\dagger \lvert 0\rangle = \lvert 1\rangle, \qquad \hat{c}^\dagger \lvert 1\rangle = 0, \]
\[ \hat{c}\lvert 1\rangle = \lvert 0\rangle, \qquad \hat{c}\lvert 0\rangle = 0, \]

but since \(n\in\{0,1\}\), one has

\[ (\hat{c}^\dagger)^2 = \hat{c}^2 = 0. \]

In matrix form for one orbital,

\[\begin{split} \hat{c} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1\\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \hat{c}^\dagger = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0\\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \hat{n}= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0\\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}. \end{split}\]

This implies

\[ \hat{c}\hat{c}^\dagger + \hat{c}^\dagger \hat{c} = \mathbb{I}, \]

i.e.

\[ \{\hat{c}^\dagger,\hat{c}\}=1. \]

More generally, fermions satisfy anticommutation relations rather than commutation relations.

Anticommutation for Different Orbitals#

For different orbitals, one must be careful because exchanging fermions changes the sign of the state.

This leads to

\[ \hat{c}_x^\dagger \hat{c}_y^\dagger = -\, \hat{c}_y^\dagger \hat{c}_x^\dagger, \]

and therefore

\[ \{\hat{c}_x^\dagger,\hat{c}_y^\dagger\}=0, \qquad \{\hat{c}_x,\hat{c}_y\}=0, \qquad \{\hat{c}_x^\dagger,\hat{c}_y\}=\delta_{xy}. \]

Summary:

  • Bosons \(\rightarrow\) commutators

  • Fermions \(\rightarrow\) anticommutators

Ordering Signs and the Jordan–Wigner String#

Because an occupation state can be built as

\[ \lvert x_1,x_2,\dots\rangle_- = \hat{c}_{x_N}^\dagger \cdots \hat{c}_{x_2}^\dagger \hat{c}_{x_1}^\dagger \lvert 0\rangle \qquad (x_1<x_2<\cdots), \]

where \(\lvert 0\rangle\) denotes the vacuum state with no particles. Changing the creation order introduces minus signs.

In particular,

\[ \hat{c}_\alpha^\dagger \lvert n_1,\dots,n_\alpha,\dots\rangle = \eta\, \lvert n_1,\dots,n_\alpha+1,\dots\rangle, \]

where the sign factor is

\[ \eta = (-1)^{\sum_{\beta<\alpha} n_\beta}. \]

This sign factor is exactly the same ordering structure that appears in the Jordan–Wigner transformation. The basic point is that when a fermion is created at site \(\alpha\), one must keep track of how many fermions occupy all sites to its left, because moving the new creation operator past those occupied sites produces a minus sign for each exchange.

This is why the fermionic Hilbert space can look very similar to a tensor product of spin-\(\tfrac12\) degrees of freedom: each orbital is either empty or occupied, just as a spin-\(\tfrac12\) is either down or up. At the level of basis states, one may therefore label fermionic configurations by binary strings such as \(\lvert 01011\rangle\), much as one would for spins.

However, the operators are different. Ordinary spin raising and lowering operators acting on different sites commute, whereas fermionic creation and annihilation operators on different sites anticommute. The Jordan–Wigner transformation makes this distinction explicit by writing a fermionic operator as a local spin flip multiplied by a nonlocal string of \(\hat{Z}\) operators that records the parity of all occupations to the left. That nonlocal string is precisely what reproduces the fermionic minus signs.

So the Hilbert spaces can be put into one-to-one correspondence, but locality and operator algebra are different: fermions map to spins only at the price of turning simple local fermionic operators into nonlocal spin operators.
